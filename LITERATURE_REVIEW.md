# Literature review: Ebola outbreaks in place and time, 1976–2026

**Scope:** a focused, source-linked review to guide an educational StoryMap, Jupyter Book, and JupyterLite Lab. Reviewed 24 November 2026. It emphasizes spatial evidence, historical interpretation, and how to avoid turning dated map snapshots into timeless facts. Full citations and reuse notes are in [REFERENCES.md](REFERENCES.md); candidate map files are in [MAP_CATALOG.md](MAP_CATALOG.md).

## A 50-year perspective

Peter Piot and Jean-Jacques Muyembe-Tamfum's [“50 years of Ebola research and response”](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01813-1), published online in *The Lancet* on 10 October 2026, is a fitting interpretive companion to this project. The article contains two first-person lectures. Piot recounts the 1976 virus identification and investigation, then connects Ebola and other epidemic responses to health systems, trust, policy, and international cooperation. Muyembe-Tamfum traces field investigation in DRC from Yambuku through Kikwit and eastern DRC, emphasizing community participation, research embedded in response, and investment in Congolese scientific institutions. These perspectives can frame the StoryMap's human and institutional history; the original field studies and dated reports remain the sources for mapped events and counts. The article and portraits are copyrighted by Elsevier and are linked rather than reproduced here.

[Jacob et al.'s “Ebola virus disease” primer](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41572-020-0147-3) offers a compact clinical and biological synthesis of Ebola virus (EBOV) transmission, diagnosis, prevention, treatment, and survivor health. Its 2020 publication date makes it useful for explaining the state of evidence around the West Africa and eastern DRC chapters. Its discussion and figures concern EBOV disease; they should not be presented as evidence that the same vaccines or treatments work against Sudan or Bundibugyo virus. The primer's outbreak map is not reused because the article page does not provide a Creative Commons license for that figure.

Muyembe-Tamfum also highlights two shifts in evidence generation: the [Guinea ring-vaccination trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)32621-6) and the [PALM therapeutic trial in DRC](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1910993). A StoryMap stop can place these studies on the timeline of response, with a link to the original trial rather than a copied figure. Their results concern Ebola virus disease caused by Ebola virus; [WHO's vaccine guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/ebola-vaccines) distinguishes that from the 2026 Bundibugyo outbreak. The map legend and accompanying notebook should preserve that species distinction.

**Date correction for the timeline:** Muyembe-Tamfum's lecture says Kikwit re-emerged in 1998. Its own reference to [Khan et al.](https://doi.org/10.1086/514306) and the [CDC chronology](https://www.cdc.gov/ebola/outbreaks/) identify the Kikwit outbreak as **1995**. Use 1995 in maps and captions, with the discrepancy recorded in the source ledger.

## What the evidence supports

The two original 1976 investigations are essential starting points. The [Sudan report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2395561/) documents a June–November outbreak centered on Nzara and Maridi, with additional cases in Tembura and Juba. The [Zaire report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2395567/) documents the Yambuku-area outbreak, including the role of health-care-associated and close-contact transmission. These were distinct outbreaks in places now called South Sudan and DRC. A StoryMap should begin with two synchronized map stops, rather than a single “origin” pin. Place names should show both historical and current country names. [WHO's current fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ebola-disease) distinguishes the Ebola, Sudan, and Bundibugyo viruses that cause major outbreaks.

The supplied scan of the Sudan investigation adds a useful historical visual: Figure 1 on printed page 252 locates Nzara, Maridi, Juba, Bumba, and Yambuku on a 1976-era Sudan/Zaire map. The report records 284 cases, including 67 in Nzara and 213 in Maridi; these are historical study counts, not coordinates for individual cases. The [Bulletin's CC BY 3.0 IGO policy](https://www.who.int/publications/journals/bulletin/about) makes the figure a candidate for a carefully credited archival panel. Retain the old country labels and explain their present-day equivalents.

For 1976 through the early 2010s, the strongest spatial synthesis located here is [Mylne et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2014.42). The authors assembled 22 outbreaks and 117 geographic transmission clusters, with a source table, supplementary maps, and downloadable GIS layers. It gives the project a defensible historical backbone for small multiples and a time-filtered map, while preserving source-by-source uncertainty. Its coverage stops before the West Africa epidemic was complete, so later chapters need additional sources. [Associated data collection](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.1168886).

The intervening decades deserve more than pins on a continent map. [Khan et al.](https://doi.org/10.1086/514306) analyze the 1995 Kikwit re-emergence; [Lamunu et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijid.2003.04.001) document response to the 2000–2001 Uganda epidemic; and [Towner et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.ppat.1000212) and [Wamala et al.](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid1607.091525) establish the 2007–2008 Bundibugyo event and virus identification. These sources support local chapter stops and prevent the 50-year story from jumping straight from 1976 to West Africa.

The [2014–2016 West Africa epidemic](https://www.who.int/emergencies/situations/ebola-outbreak-2014-2016-west-africa) calls for a different scale: national markers alone obscure spread among districts, cross-border movement, and the timing of urban transmission. [Gire et al. (2014)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/pmid/25214632/) provides genomic evidence relevant to early regional transmission, while the WHO overview anchors the historical sequence. An archival [CDC map dated 17 December 2014](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2014_West_Africa_Ebola_virus_outbreak_situation_map.jpg) can show what was known at one moment; it must be captioned as that date's snapshot. Comparing successive dated maps is more honest than animating an undated final map backward.

[Rainisch et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.3201/eid2103.141845) and [Kramer et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160294) study regional spread using spatial models. Their results can motivate a notebook that compares distance, population, and reported cases, while making clear that a fitted transmission model is an inference rather than a direct observation of every movement.

The [2018–2020 eastern DRC outbreak](https://www.cdc.gov/ebola/outbreaks/) and the overlapping [2020 Équateur outbreak](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ECDM_20200807_DRC_Ebola_update.pdf) need separate event IDs and colors. The license-checked ERCC maps from [October 2018](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20181031_Map_DRC_NordKivu_Ebola_DailyMap.pdf) and [May 2019](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20190522_DailyMap_DRC_NordKivu_Ebola2.pdf) provide dated visual checkpoints. The StoryMap should explain why two outbreaks in the same country and overlapping years are not one continuous geographic event.

[Shears and Garavan (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infpip.2020.100038) discuss the eastern DRC response in the context of conflict and community trust. Their review is useful narrative context for the maps; dated WHO and CDC reports should remain the source of mapped counts.

The [2022–2023 Uganda outbreak](https://www.who.int/emergencies/situations/ebola-uganda-2022) and the [2025 Uganda outbreak](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2025-DON566) were caused by Sudan virus, whereas the [2025 DRC outbreak](https://www.cdc.gov/ebola/outbreaks/) was caused by Ebola virus. This distinction is analytically important for the timeline, legends, and explanations of vaccines and therapeutics. An [ERCC Uganda map with data as of 12 November 2022](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20221115_Uganda_Ebola_Outbreak.png) is a reusable visual for the earlier episode, but it does not represent final 2023 totals.

The 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak is **ongoing**. The [WHO report published 20 November 2026](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON630) reports 11,613 confirmed cases and 5,678 deaths in DRC as of 17 November, across 68 health zones in eight provinces, with confirmed cases also reported outside DRC since May. These figures are a dated snapshot, not an endpoint. The [WHO situation-report index](https://afro.who.int/health-topics/ebola-disease/outbreak-drc-26) should be used for updates. A [June 2026 ERCC map](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ECDM_20260604_DRC_Ebola_outbreak(1).pdf) can show the outbreak's early geography, and the [Ituri map](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Map_of_the_Ituri_Province_Ebola_outbreak.svg) can support a later health-zone view if a fixed revision and its data date are recorded.

[Haider, Khan, and Rahman's 2026 editorial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijid.2026.108843) frames the outbreak around diagnostic delays, surveillance capacity, and the need for Bundibugyo-specific countermeasures. Its Figure 1 combines a selected-outbreak timeline and continental locator, with CDC and WHO named as underlying sources. The editorial was written early in the outbreak and should not supply current counts or a final 2026 map. Its text also contains an apparent “2013–2026” date error for the West Africa epidemic; the [WHO event history](https://www.who.int/emergencies/situations/ebola-outbreak-2014-2016-west-africa) supplies the correct 2014–2016 response period. The figure is [CC BY-NC-ND 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/) under the PDF's stated license, so any reuse must remain unchanged and meet its noncommercial and attribution conditions.

[Palmer et al.'s September 2026 cross-border brief](https://doi.org/10.19088/MHRN.2026.001) adds social and geographic context for the DRC–South Sudan–Uganda borderlands. It treats borders as networks of crossings, markets, clinics, kinship, and trade, and explains why formal border posts alone cannot describe mobility or preparedness. The brief says South Sudan had reported no cases **as of its 13 August 2026 writing date**; that is a dated statement, not a current status claim. For the StoryMap, distinguish preparedness corridors and qualitative mobility from confirmed transmission routes, and avoid plotting exact informal paths or individual movements from this narrative. Its three maps are attributed to IMPACT Initiatives, WHO, and IOM DTM as “reproduced with permission”; despite the brief's CC BY 4.0 text license, those map figures need their own reuse clearance. [Map catalog](MAP_CATALOG.md#third-party-maps-in-the-cross-border-brief).

## A map that must be explained carefully

[Pigott et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.04395) maps the **zoonotic niche**: modeled environmental suitability and evidence of potential animal-to-human emergence. It is valuable for discussing ecology and uncertainty, but it is not a map of observed human cases. Put it beside, not underneath or in place of, a human-outbreak layer. Its model period and assumptions should appear in the caption. This is a useful chapter about what different kinds of maps can and cannot tell us.

## Implications for the StoryMap and notebooks

1. Use a typed event table with `event_id`, virus species, date interval, location name, geography type, source, report date, case definition, and confidence/precision. Keep exact points, approximate settlements, districts, health zones, and countries visually distinct.
2. Show a **report-time slider** for evolving outbreaks. Display “known as of” rather than retroactively giving readers future information on an earlier stop.
3. Give each visual a short source line and link to the underlying report. Show counts only when the numerator and denominator come from the same report and case definition.
4. Use small multiples for cross-period comparison. A 3D terrain view may orient readers but should not imply that elevation explains transmission without evidence.
5. Pair historical images and videos with captions describing their date, creator, and rights. Link the [CDC Museum timeline](https://cdcmuseum.org/exhibits/show/ebola/item/7) as an interpretive resource; its displayed images have not been cleared for copying into this repo.

## Limits of this review

This is a curated starting bibliography, not a systematic review or a complete line list. Some older counts differ among sources because suspected/probable/confirmed definitions and retrospective corrections differ. The 2026 situation is changing. The project should preserve a versioned source record for every dataset and map revision it eventually publishes.
