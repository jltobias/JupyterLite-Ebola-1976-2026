# 1976: two investigations

The first chapter needs two places on the map. The [Sudan field investigation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2395561/) documented cases around Nzara, Maridi, Tembura, and Juba between June and November 1976, in territory now called South Sudan. The [Zaire investigation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2395567/) documented a separate outbreak around Yambuku in the present-day DRC. The viruses later became known as Sudan virus and Ebola virus. A single “Ebola origin” marker would hide both the geographic separation and the distinct viruses. [WHO disease overview](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ebola-disease)

The original reports explain how investigators reconstructed early transmission with interviews, clinical observations, laboratory work, and local geography. The Yambuku report describes amplification through unsafe injections and close contact; the Sudan report describes spread across towns and health facilities. These findings should be read in their historical setting, including the reports' older country names and case definitions.

[Pattyn and colleagues' 1977 report](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(77)92002-5) documents early virus isolation from a Yambuku-associated case. Its “Marburg-like” wording records what investigators could identify at the time. [Feldmann and Geisbert's 2011 review](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)60667-8) offers a later clinical and virological perspective; neither article supplies the coordinates of people affected in 1976.

## Historical map visual

Figure 1 of the [1978 Sudan paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2395561/) is an archival location map with Nzara, Maridi, Juba, Bumba, and Yambuku. Its printed page 252 shows the geography available to investigators then. The [Bulletin of the World Health Organization's archive policy](https://www.who.int/publications/journals/bulletin/about) states CC BY 3.0 IGO for archived articles; check the figure for any separate credit before reproducing it. A modern locator should explain that “Sudan” and “Zaire” on this map correspond to today's South Sudan and DRC in the areas discussed here.

## What the map can say

The tour's Nzara/Maridi and Yambuku pins represent **named areas**, not individual patient locations. A historical figure can establish where an investigation took place; it cannot, by itself, prove exact household locations or a single route between the two outbreaks. The [StoryMap](https://jltobias.github.io/JupyterLite-Ebola-1976-2026-StoryMaps/) lets readers move between both stops while keeping 1976 active on the timeline.

For a first-person 50-year perspective, read [Piot and Muyembe-Tamfum's anniversary lectures](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(26)01813-1). Their accounts are interpretive context; the two original reports remain the sources for mapped 1976 events. The lectures' pages and portraits are copyrighted and are linked here.
